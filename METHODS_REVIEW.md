# Methods review: Baumol (1982)

**Paper.** William J. Baumol, "Contestable Markets: An Uprising in the Theory of Industry Structure," *American
Economic Review* 72(1), March 1982, pp. 1–15 (the author's presidential address to the American Economic
Association). Companion book: W. J. Baumol, J. C. Panzar and R. D. Willig, *Contestable Markets and the Theory of
Industry Structure*, Harcourt Brace Jovanovich, 1982.

**Statistical focus.** The two formal claims the paper is known for and that carry its policy weight: that in a
perfectly contestable market no excess profit or inefficiency can persist and, with two or more efficient firms,
price equals marginal cost; and the applied claim that airline city-pair markets approximate contestability, so that
potential entrants discipline fares regardless of how many carriers fly a route.

**Review standard.** Internal consistency of the formal results, explicitness of assumptions, and the testability of
the predictions; for the empirical airline claims cited in support, standard reporting of estimates, uncertainty and
sample construction.

**What was read.** The 1982 *AER* article itself was not available to me (it is paywalled on JSTOR, and I did not
use unauthorized copies). I read its bibliographic records (IDEAS/RePEc) and, in full, the authors' own
restatement of the theory and its airline application in Elizabeth E. Bailey and William J. Baumol, "Deregulation
and the Theory of Contestable Markets," *Yale Journal on Regulation* 1: 111 (1984), open access from the Yale Law
School repository. Page references below are to that 1984 article. I also read the abstract of Goolsbee and
Syverson (2008, NBER Working Paper 11072). Statements about what the 1982 article contains are limited to what the
1984 restatement, written by the same author two years later and citing the 1982 work, attributes to the theory.

## Methods summary

**Research design.** Deductive economic theory. The paper defines an idealized market structure (perfect
contestability) as an alternative benchmark to perfect competition and derives its equilibrium properties. There is
no data collection, estimation or experiment. Empirical content enters through illustrative discussion of
industries, chiefly airlines after the 1978 Airline Deregulation Act, and through references to studies by others
(Bailey and Panzar 1981; Civil Aeronautics Board staff reports).

**Sampling strategy, sample size, inclusion and exclusion criteria.** Not applicable to the theory. The supporting
airline evidence cited in the 1984 restatement (Bailey and Panzar 1981, Graham, Kaplan and Sibley 1982) is described
qualitatively, without the samples, periods or route counts behind it.

**Independent and dependent variables.** In the theory, the primitives are the cost function (identical for
incumbents and entrants), market demand, and the absence of sunk costs; the outcomes are prices, profits, the number
and size of firms, and cross-subsidy. In the airline application the implicit dependent variable is the fare on a
city-pair route and the independent variables are the number of actual carriers and the presence of potential
entrants.

**Statistical methods.** None. The "statistics" of the paper are its formal results: a definition, an equilibrium
(sustainability) condition and propositions derived from them.

**Reported effect sizes, confidence intervals, p-values.** None reported for the theory. The cited empirical claims
are reported as directions ("effectively policing," "a significant role," "a substantial effect") without point
estimates, standard errors or test statistics in the text I read.

**Missing data treatment.** Not applicable.

**Stated limitations.** The authors describe perfect contestability as "unattainable except as an approximation"
(p. 112), concede that the
assumption of sticky incumbent prices fails in aviation, that actual competition has mattered in virtually all
airline markets since the first years of deregulation (p. 129), and that sunk costs exist at airports, in landing
slots and long-term leases of airport space (p. 131). They also note that the theory of imperfect ("workably") contestable markets was then
only beginning (p. 122).

## Interpretation notes

- The formal results are conditional on three strong assumptions stated by the authors: no sunk costs, identical
  cost functions for incumbents and entrants, and a pool of potential entrants. On a fourth, incumbent prices that
  respond slowly to entry, the restatement is not consistent: it calls slow responses conducive to contestability but
  not essential (p. 121), then lists sticky incumbent prices as a condition the airline market fails (p. 129). The pricing result follows cleanly from these, and its proof is internally consistent as
  presented.
- The theory's testable prediction for airlines is sharp: holding cost fixed, the number of carriers actually flying
  a route should not affect fares once potential entrants exist. The 1984 restatement already reports that the
  evidence ran against this outside the earliest deregulation period, so the airline claim is best read as a
  hypothesis the authors themselves qualified.
- The applied claims rest on studies summarized in words. Without their estimates, samples and uncertainty, a
  reader of the restatement cannot judge how strong the early supporting evidence was. This is a reporting
  limitation of a policy article, not evidence of error.
- Later published work tested the potential-entry channel directly. Goolsbee and Syverson (2008) report that
  incumbents cut fares significantly when Southwest becomes a likely entrant, and that more than half of Southwest's
  total effect on incumbents' fares precedes its entry, which supports a role for potential competition by one
  carrier without supporting perfect contestability.
- I did not find a published correction to the 1982 article. The Schwartz and Reynolds (1983) comment and the Baumol,
  Panzar and Willig (1983) reply dispute the theory's robustness to entry lags, which is a disagreement about
  assumptions, not a documented analytical error.

## Unanswered questions

- How large must sunk costs or entry lags be before contestable outcomes break down? The 1984 restatement says this
  work was only beginning.
- Which costs of entering an airline route are sunk in practice (gate leases, slots, marketing, frequent-flyer
  programs), and how much each matters for fares.
- Whether some potential entrants (low-cost carriers) discipline fares while others (network carriers present at
  both ends) do not, and why.
- What samples, periods and specifications stood behind the early airline evidence the authors cite.

## Items requiring expert verification

- The exact wording, propositions and any numerical examples in the 1982 *AER* article, which I did not read; the
  extraction above relies on the 1984 restatement by the same author.
- Whether the 1982 address itself presents airline evidence or leaves it to the book and later articles.
- The estimates in Bailey and Panzar (1981) and in the Civil Aeronautics Board staff reports cited at p. 129.
- How the modern rerun in this repository relates to the original: it does not reproduce anything in the paper,
  which has no data. It tests the theory's airline prediction on 2014 to 2024 ticket data and finds that the number
  of airlines actually flying a route predicts fares, that airlines merely present at both endpoints exert little
  measurable pressure, and that Southwest's arrival at both endpoints is followed by incumbent fare cuts before it
  enters, in line with Goolsbee and Syverson.
